### WHAT THIS COURSE IS AND WHO IT'S FOR (1:15–3:00)

ISM2411 is the entry point to programming in the Information Systems major. No prior coding experience is assumed — if you've never written a line of code, you are exactly who this course is built for. If you've used Excel or Google Sheets to work with data, that instinct will help you; we're taking the same kind of thinking and giving you a more powerful tool.

Every concept in this course is taught through a business lens — pricing formulas, sales transactions, customer records, retail datasets. You won't write toy programs that print "Hello World" and stop there. You'll load real-feeling retail data, clean it, analyze it, and chart it, the same way an analyst would on the job.

By the end of the semester you will have: 15 completed labs on GitHub, five DataCamp course completions, and a capstone retail sales analysis you can show a recruiter. That GitHub portfolio is not a side effect of the course — it's one of the deliverables.

### TOUR: THE SYLLABUS (8:00–13:00)

Click into the **Syllabus** page now. I'm not going to read it to you line by line — you need to read the whole thing yourself before Module 2 — but here's what actually matters day to day.

**Format.** This is a hybrid course. Reading and lecture content is online, self-paced, done before lab. Lab is in person, once a week, hands-on, with me there for real-time help. Module 1 — this week — has no lab meeting; it's setup and orientation only. The weekly in-person lab starts in Module 2.

**How your grade breaks down:**
- Weekly Labs — 30%. Fourteen labs, due Sunday at 11:59 PM, graded on correctness, completion, code quality, and proper submission.
- Weekly Quizzes — 5%. Fourteen quizzes on Canvas, open-note, tied to that week's reading.
- DataCamp — 15%. Five required courses, graded on completion, not score. Two optional courses are worth up to 5% extra credit.
- Midterm Exam — 20%. Module 9, in class, 75 minutes, one double-sided cheatsheet allowed, no laptops or phones.
- Capstone Project — 25%. Module 16, an end-to-end retail analysis submitted as a GitHub repo with a live 5-to-7-minute presentation.
- Participation — 5%.

**The policies most likely to affect you:**
- **Late work:** labs lose 10% per day, late up to three days, then a zero. You get one 48-hour no-questions-asked extension per semester — email me before the deadline to use it. It doesn't cover the midterm or capstone.
- **Group work:** all submitted code is individual. Talking through concepts and debugging strategies with classmates is fine and encouraged; sharing code — screen, file, or chat — is academic dishonesty. If you and a classmate debug together, you each still write your own solution from scratch afterward.
- **AI policy:** this course teaches disclosed AI use, not avoidance of it. Three modes: **Explainer** — always fine, ask AI to explain a concept or a piece of code. **Debugger** — fine, but read the traceback yourself first. **Generator** — allowed only if you understand every line, can explain it without the AI, and disclose it using the exact language on the AI Policy page. Undisclosed AI-generated code, or AI-generated code you can't explain, is academic dishonesty. AI use of any kind on the midterm is not allowed.
- **Communication:** check Canvas at least twice a week. Email gets a response within 48 business hours on weekdays — messages after 5 PM Friday get answered by end of day Monday. Include your name, U Number, and section in every email. For code questions, paste the actual error text, not a screenshot.

Full detail on every one of these — plus the grading scale, the module-by-module schedule, and the university-wide policies on Title IX, academic integrity, and accommodations — is on the syllabus page. Read it this week. There will be a syllabus quiz on Canvas due by the end of Week 1 that confirms you did, and it also counts as your official first-day attendance record.

### MEET DR. REED — ASK ME ANYTHING (13:00–17:30)

Now, the part where you actually get to know who's teaching this.

[Instructor: open live to camera — who you are, your background, and why you teach this course the way you do. Then move into the questions below.]

**"I've never coded before — am I going to be okay in this class?"**
Yes. This course is built assuming zero prior experience, and most students in this room have never written a line of code before Module 1. The pacing, the labs, and the office hours all exist because of that assumption, not despite it. What matters is that you do the reading before lab, you run your code often instead of writing twenty lines and hoping, and you come to office hours with a specific error instead of "I'm stuck" — I can work with a specific error in five minutes; "I'm stuck" takes much longer to untangle.

**"What's the best way to reach you, and how fast will you respond?"**
Canvas Mail or my USF email — not a DM, not a comment on a discussion post. I respond within 48 business hours on weekdays; if you email me Friday evening, expect an answer by end of day Monday, not over the weekend. Include your U Number and section number every time — it's on the syllabus, and it's the fastest way to get a fast answer instead of a "who is this" back-and-forth.

**"What if I'm behind, or I think I'm going to fail?"**
Talk to me before it happens, not after. The one 48-hour extension exists for exactly this — a rough week, a real conflict. The earlier you tell me, the more room I have to work with. What I can't do is retroactively fix a missed deadline after the fact with no communication beforehand — that's not favoritism, it's just how the late policy has to work for everyone.

**"What's your grading philosophy?"**
I grade what you actually submit against a clear rubric — correctness, completion, code quality, and proper submission for labs; the published breakdown for everything else. I'm not grading on effort you told me about but didn't show in the code. If you disagree with a grade, you have one week from when it posts in Canvas to raise it — after that window, it's final.

**"Office hours — when, and what should I bring?"**
Monday and Wednesday, 1:30 to 3 PM, in CIS 2070B. Can't make it in person? Check Canvas for the Teams link and join remotely. Bring your code, the full error message or traceback, and a one-sentence description of what you already tried. That's the fastest path to actually learning something in that session, instead of me just handing you the fix.
